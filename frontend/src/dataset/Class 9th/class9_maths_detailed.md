## Chapter 1: Orienting Yourself: The Use of Coordinates

**Concept:** Introduces the Cartesian coordinate system — a way to describe any point on a flat plane using two numbers (x, y), based on distance from two perpendicular reference lines (the x-axis and y-axis).

**Key facts:**
- The plane is divided into 4 quadrants by the x-axis and y-axis.
- Quadrant I: (+, +), Quadrant II: (−, +), Quadrant III: (−, −), Quadrant IV: (+, −)
- The point where the axes cross is called the origin: (0, 0).

**Worked Example:** In which quadrant does the point (−3, 5) lie?
x is negative, y is positive → Quadrant II.

**Common mistake:** Reversing x and y when plotting — (2, 5) and (5, 2) are different points.

## Chapter 2: Introduction to Linear Polynomials

**Concept:** A polynomial is an algebraic expression with variables and coefficients. A linear polynomial has degree 1 (the highest power of the variable is 1), in the form ax + b.

**Key facts:**
- Degree of a polynomial = the highest power of the variable.
- A zero (or root) of a polynomial p(x) is a value of x where p(x) = 0.
- For a linear polynomial ax + b, the zero is x = −b/a.

**Worked Example:** Find the zero of the polynomial 3x − 12.
Set 3x − 12 = 0 → 3x = 12 → x = 4.

**Common mistake:** Confusing the "zero" of a polynomial (a value of x) with the constant term (a fixed number in the expression) — they are different things.

## Chapter 3: The World of Numbers (Real Numbers)

**Concept:** Covers rational and irrational numbers together as "real numbers," including proofs of irrationality and geometric representation.

**Key facts:**
- Rational numbers: expressible as p/q (terminating or repeating decimals).
- Irrational numbers: cannot be expressed as p/q (non-terminating, non-repeating decimals) — e.g., √2, π.
- √2 is proved irrational using a proof by contradiction.
- The "square root spiral" is a geometric construction that visually represents √2, √3, √4... using right triangles.
- Between any two real numbers, infinitely many other real numbers exist (density property).

**Worked Example:** Is 0.101001000100001... (with an increasing number of zeros each time) rational or irrational?
It's irrational — the decimal is non-terminating and does not repeat in a fixed pattern.

**Common mistake:** Assuming all square roots are irrational — √4 = 2, which is rational, since 4 is a perfect square.

## Chapter 4: Exploring Algebraic Identities

**Concept:** Extends algebraic identities from Class 8, applying them to factorization and simplification of more complex expressions, including visual/geometric proofs.

**Key formulas:**
- (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
- (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
- a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)
- (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a+b)x + ab
- a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
- a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)

**Worked Example:** Factorize x² − 49.
Using a² − b² = (a+b)(a−b): x² − 49 = x² − 7² = (x+7)(x−7).

**Common mistake:** Trying to factorize a² + b² using the difference-of-squares identity — that identity only applies to a² − b² (subtraction), not addition.

## Chapter 5: I'm Up and Down, and Round and Round (Circles)

**Concept:** Explores properties of circles — chords, arcs, angles, and the relationships between them.

**Key facts:**
- Equal chords of a circle subtend equal angles at the centre.
- The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
- Angle subtended by an arc at the centre is twice the angle subtended at any point on the remaining part of the circle.
- Angles in the same segment of a circle are equal.
- The angle in a semicircle is always 90°.

**Worked Example:** A chord subtends an angle of 70° at the centre. What angle does it subtend at a point on the major arc?
Angle at the centre = 2 × angle at the circumference → 70° = 2 × angle → angle = 35°.

**Common mistake:** Assuming the angle-doubling rule applies regardless of which arc the point is on — the point must be on the arc "remaining" after removing the chord's arc, not on the same arc as the chord itself.

## Chapter 6: Measuring Space: Perimeter and Area

**Concept:** Builds on Class 8 mensuration with more complex or combined shapes, and introduces Heron's formula for triangle area.

**Key formulas:**
- Heron's Formula: Area = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)], where s = (a+b+c)/2 (semi-perimeter) and a, b, c are the triangle's sides.
- Useful when the height of a triangle isn't directly known.

**Worked Example:** Find the area of a triangle with sides 5 cm, 6 cm, and 7 cm.
s = (5+6+7)/2 = 9. Area = √[9(9−5)(9−6)(9−7)] = √[9×4×3×2] = √216 ≈ 14.7 cm².

**Common mistake:** Forgetting to compute the semi-perimeter (s) first before applying Heron's formula, or using the full perimeter instead of half of it.

## Chapter 7: The Mathematics of Maybe: Introduction to Probability

**Concept:** Probability measures how likely an event is to occur, expressed as a number between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain).

**Key formula:** P(event) = (Number of favourable outcomes) / (Total number of possible outcomes)

**Worked Example:** What is the probability of rolling a 4 on a standard 6-sided die?
Favourable outcomes = 1 (just the number 4). Total outcomes = 6. P(rolling a 4) = 1/6.

**Common mistake:** Adding probabilities of unrelated events incorrectly, or forgetting that the probability of an event and its complement (not happening) must add up to 1.

**Practice question:** A bag has 4 red balls and 6 blue balls. What's the probability of picking a red ball at random? (Answer: 4/10 = 2/5)

## Chapter 8: Predicting What Comes Next?: Exploring Sequences and Progressions

**Concept:** A sequence is an ordered list of numbers following a rule. An Arithmetic Progression (AP) has a constant difference between terms; a Geometric Progression (GP) has a constant ratio between terms.

**Key formulas:**
- AP: nth term = a + (n−1)d, where a = first term, d = common difference.
- GP: nth term = a × r^(n−1), where a = first term, r = common ratio.

**Worked Example (AP):** Find the 10th term of the sequence 3, 7, 11, 15...
a = 3, d = 4. nth term = a + (n−1)d = 3 + (10−1)(4) = 3 + 36 = 39.

**Worked Example (GP):** Find the 5th term of the sequence 2, 6, 18, 54...
a = 2, r = 3. 5th term = 2 × 3⁴ = 2 × 81 = 162.

**Common mistake:** Using the AP formula on a GP sequence (or vice versa) — always check whether consecutive terms have a constant *difference* (AP) or constant *ratio* (GP) before choosing the formula.

**Real-world connection:** This chapter also touches on patterns like fractals and the Tower of Hanoi puzzle, showing how sequences model real recursive problems.
