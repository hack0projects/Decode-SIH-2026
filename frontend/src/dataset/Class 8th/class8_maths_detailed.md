# Class 8 Maths — Ganita Prakash (Part 1 & 2) — Detailed Chapter Content

## Chapter 1: A Square and A Cube

**Concept:** A square number is what you get when you multiply a number by itself (n × n = n²). A cube number is what you get when you multiply a number by itself three times (n × n × n = n³).

**Key facts:**
- Square numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36... (1², 2², 3², 4², 5², 6²...)
- Cube numbers: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125... (1³, 2³, 3³, 4³, 5³...)
- Square root (√): the reverse of squaring. √25 = 5, since 5² = 25.
- Cube root (∛): the reverse of cubing. ∛27 = 3, since 3³ = 27.
- A number ending in 2, 3, 7, or 8 can never be a perfect square.

**Worked Example:** Is 225 a perfect square? Find its square root.
225 = 15 × 15 = 15². Yes, it's a perfect square, and √225 = 15.

**Common mistake:** Confusing "squaring" with "doubling" — squaring a number means multiplying it by itself, not by 2. 5² = 25, not 10.

**Practice question:** Find the smallest number by which 72 must be multiplied to make it a perfect square. (Hint: find the prime factorization first — 72 = 2³ × 3². The power of 2 is odd, so multiply by 2. Answer: 2, giving 144 = 12².)

## Chapter 2: Power Play (Exponents & Powers)

**Concept:** Exponents are shorthand for repeated multiplication. In aⁿ, "a" is the base and "n" is the exponent/power.

**Key formulas (Laws of Exponents):**
- aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
- aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
- (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
- a⁰ = 1 (any non-zero number raised to power 0 is 1)
- a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (negative exponent means reciprocal)
- Standard form: a number written as k × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ k < 10 (used for very large/small numbers, e.g., the speed of light ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s)

**Worked Example:** Simplify 2³ × 2⁴.
Using the law aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ: 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128.

**Common mistake:** Writing aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐˣⁿ (multiplying the exponents instead of adding them) — this rule only applies to (aᵐ)ⁿ, not aᵐ × aⁿ.

**Practice question:** Express 45,000,000 in standard form. (Answer: 4.5 × 10⁷)

## Chapter 3: A Story of Numbers (Rational Numbers)

**Concept:** A rational number is any number that can be written as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0. This includes whole numbers, fractions, and terminating/repeating decimals.

**Key facts:**
- Rational numbers can be represented on a number line, between integers.
- Between any two rational numbers, there are infinitely many other rational numbers.
- Operations: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of rational numbers follow the same sign rules as integers.

**Worked Example:** Add -3/4 and 5/6.
Find LCM of 4 and 6 = 12. Convert: -3/4 = -9/12, 5/6 = 10/12. Sum = (-9+10)/12 = 1/12.

**Common mistake:** Adding numerators and denominators directly (e.g., treating 1/2 + 1/3 as 2/5) instead of finding a common denominator first.

**Practice question:** Find a rational number between 1/3 and 1/2. (One valid answer: 5/12)

## Chapter 4: Understanding Quadrilaterals

**Concept:** A quadrilateral is any closed shape with 4 sides. Different types have different properties based on their sides, angles, and diagonals.

**Key facts:**
- The sum of interior angles of any quadrilateral = 360°.
- Parallelogram: opposite sides equal and parallel, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisect each other.
- Rectangle: a parallelogram with all angles = 90°.
- Rhombus: a parallelogram with all sides equal, diagonals bisect each other at 90°.
- Square: a rectangle and a rhombus at once — all sides equal, all angles 90°.
- Trapezium: only one pair of opposite sides is parallel.

**Worked Example:** In a quadrilateral, three angles are 90°, 85°, and 95°. Find the fourth angle.
Sum of all four angles = 360°. Fourth angle = 360 − (90+85+95) = 360 − 270 = 90°.

**Common mistake:** Assuming all parallelograms are rectangles — a parallelogram only becomes a rectangle if its angles are all 90°.

## Chapter 5: Number Play

**Concept:** This chapter builds pattern recognition using number puzzles, magic squares, and digit-based reasoning — a foundation for algebraic thinking rather than a specific formula-driven topic.

**Example activity:** In a 3×3 magic square, every row, column, and diagonal must sum to the same number. Recognizing and constructing such patterns strengthens logical reasoning skills used later in algebra.

**Common mistake:** Trying to solve number-pattern puzzles by guesswork alone instead of identifying the underlying rule (e.g., checking whether a sequence increases by a constant difference, a constant ratio, or a more complex rule).

## Chapter 6: We Distribute, Yet Things Multiply (Algebraic Expressions & Identities)

**Concept:** This chapter covers how multiplication distributes over addition/subtraction in algebra, and introduces key algebraic identities, often visualized using area models of rectangles/squares.

**Key formulas (Identities):**
- (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
- (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
- (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
- Distributive law: a(b + c) = ab + ac

**Worked Example:** Expand (x + 5)².
Using (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b²: (x+5)² = x² + 2(x)(5) + 5² = x² + 10x + 25.

**Common mistake:** Writing (a+b)² = a² + b² — forgetting the middle "2ab" term entirely.

**Practice question:** Expand (3x − 4)². (Answer: 9x² − 24x + 16)

## Chapter 7: Proportional Reasoning – 1

**Concept:** A proportion states that two ratios are equal: a/b = c/d. This chapter introduces the idea before Chapter 10 covers direct/inverse proportion in depth.

**Key formula:** If a/b = c/d, then a × d = b × c (cross-multiplication).

**Worked Example:** If 4 books cost ₹200, how much do 7 books cost?
Set up proportion: 4/200 = 7/x → 4x = 1400 → x = 350. So 7 books cost ₹350.

**Common mistake:** Setting up the ratio upside down (e.g., putting cost/books instead of books/cost consistently on both sides).

---

## Chapter 8: Fractions in Disguise

**Concept:** Covers operations on fractions and rational expressions that don't immediately look like simple fractions — such as fractions within fractions, or expressions requiring simplification before operating.

**Worked Example:** Simplify (2/3 + 1/6) ÷ (5/6).
First: 2/3 + 1/6 = 4/6 + 1/6 = 5/6. Then: (5/6) ÷ (5/6) = 1.

**Common mistake:** Dividing fractions by dividing numerators and denominators directly instead of multiplying by the reciprocal (a/b ÷ c/d = a/b × d/c).

## Chapter 9: The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem

**Concept:** In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (the longest side, opposite the right angle) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

**Key formula:** a² + b² = c² (where c is the hypotenuse)

**Worked Example:** A right triangle has legs of length 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.
c² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 → c = √100 = 10 cm.

**Historical note:** This relationship was described by the ancient Indian mathematician Baudhāyana in the Sulba Sutras before being popularized in the West as the "Pythagoras theorem."

**Common mistake:** Applying a² + b² = c² to any triangle — this only works for right-angled triangles, and c must specifically be the hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle), not just any side.

## Chapter 10: Proportional Reasoning – 2 (Direct & Inverse Proportion)

**Concept:**
- **Direct proportion:** as one quantity increases, the other increases at the same rate (y = kx, for some constant k). Example: more hours worked → more wages earned.
- **Inverse proportion:** as one quantity increases, the other decreases proportionally (xy = k, constant). Example: more workers → less time needed to finish a job.

**Worked Example (Direct):** If 5 pens cost ₹75, find the cost of 8 pens.
Cost per pen = 75/5 = ₹15. Cost of 8 pens = 8 × 15 = ₹120.

**Worked Example (Inverse):** 6 workers can finish a wall in 10 days. How many days will 4 workers take?
6 × 10 = 4 × x → x = 60/4 = 15 days.

**Common mistake:** Using direct-proportion logic on an inverse-proportion problem (e.g., assuming fewer workers means fewer days, when it actually means more days).

## Chapter 11: Exploring Some Geometric Themes

**Concept:** Covers geometric constructions (using compass and ruler) and deeper relationships between shapes, building spatial reasoning beyond basic classification.

**Example skill:** Constructing a quadrilateral when given specific side lengths and angles, using step-by-step compass-and-ruler methods rather than freehand drawing.

**Common mistake:** Attempting geometric constructions without first sketching a rough figure to plan the steps — this often leads to construction errors that are hard to spot afterward.

## Chapter 12: Tales by Dots and Lines (Graphs & Coordinate Geometry)

**Concept:** Introduces plotting points on a coordinate plane (x-axis and y-axis) and interpreting graphs.

**Key facts:**
- A point is written as (x, y), where x is the horizontal distance and y is the vertical distance from the origin (0,0).
- A straight-line graph often represents a direct proportion relationship.

**Worked Example:** Plot the point (3, 4) and describe its position.
Starting at the origin, move 3 units right (positive x) and 4 units up (positive y).

**Common mistake:** Reversing the order in coordinates — (3, 4) and (4, 3) are different points; x always comes first.

## Chapter 13: Algebra Play (Linear Equations)

**Concept:** Covers forming and solving linear equations in one variable — equations where the unknown appears only to the power of 1.

**Key method:** To solve, isolate the variable by performing the same operation on both sides of the equation.

**Worked Example:** Solve 3x + 7 = 22.
Subtract 7 from both sides: 3x = 15. Divide both sides by 3: x = 5.

**Common mistake:** Performing an operation on only one side of the equation (e.g., subtracting 7 from the left side but forgetting to subtract it from the right side too).

**Practice question:** Solve 2(x − 3) = 10. (Answer: x = 8)

## Chapter 14: Area

**Concept:** Covers formulas for measuring the 2D space enclosed by shapes, and surface area/volume for solids.

**Key formulas:**
- Rectangle: Area = length × breadth
- Square: Area = side²
- Triangle: Area = ½ × base × height
- Parallelogram: Area = base × height
- Trapezium: Area = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × height
- Circle: Area = πr²
- Cube: Volume = side³, Total Surface Area = 6 × side²
- Cuboid: Volume = length × breadth × height
- Cylinder: Volume = πr²h, Curved Surface Area = 2πrh

**Worked Example:** Find the area of a trapezium with parallel sides 8 cm and 12 cm, and height 5 cm.
Area = ½ × (8+12) × 5 = ½ × 20 × 5 = 50 cm².

**Common mistake:** Using the slant height instead of the perpendicular (vertical) height in area formulas — only the perpendicular height gives the correct area.
